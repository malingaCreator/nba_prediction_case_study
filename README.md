# Next-Best-Action Engine for Retail Banking

An ML-powered next-best-action system: for a given customer in a given month,
choose **one** action — a product offer, a servicing nudge, or **nothing at
all** — plus a channel, subject to eligibility, consent, and contact-frequency
rules.

Built end-to-end: problem framing → propensity models → rules-gated
arbitration → fairness audit → working demo.

**[Read the case study](case-study.md)** · **[Try the demo](#)** ·
**[See the framing doc](framing.md)** *(written before any model was built)*

## The recommendation

**Ready on ranking quality. Not ready on outcome parity. I would not ship
this.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Ranking** | MAP@1 **0.844** vs. 0.689 popularity baseline — **+22.4%** |
| **Consent compliance** | **0 violations** across 48,237 decisions |
| **Suppression** | **70%** of customers get no contact — by design |
| **Fairness** | Recommendation accuracy varies **2.32× across age bands** |

The model beats a properly-matched baseline, its probabilities are calibrated,
and the consent gate holds. It still fails the guardrail that matters: when
the system contacts a 31–45 year-old the recommendation lands 6.9% of the
time; for an over-60 customer, 3.0%. That is not differential targeting, which
can be legitimate — it is the system serving some groups worse when it reaches
them.

Age was **excluded from the model's features**. Tenure and an activity flag
reconstruct it anyway. Excluding a protected attribute removes the most direct
path, not the correlation structure — which is why the attribute stayed in the
*measurement*. Had it been dropped from both, as is common, this would have
shipped with the gap intact.

## How it works

```
candidate actions
      │
      ▼
[ RULES ]  eligibility · consent · frequency cap      ← hard gate, auditable
      │
      ▼
[  ML   ]  rank survivors by expected value            ← learned, monitored
      │
      ▼
[ ARBITRATION ]  compare across action types, incl. "none"
      │
      ▼
   one action + channel
```

A model score can never override a rule. The frequency cap is checked before
any probability is read, and every decision carries a trace of what was
dropped and why — explainability without an LLM in the decision path.

`No action` is a first-class outcome. A contact costs attention, goodwill, and
eventually the channel itself.

## Three findings worth the click

**Expected-value arbitration concentrates by construction.** Direct debit
takes 62% of all contacts. Tested against three different value regimes —
equal values, halved, doubled — and the concentration survives all of them
(55–61%). Predicted probabilities span ~100× across products while values span
~5×, so the high-variance term decides the ranking. Pure EV optimises for what
is most *predictable*, not most *valuable*. Portfolio balance needs an
explicit mechanism and an owner.

**Screen product volume by month, not in total.** Two products showed adequate
totals and were in terminal decline by the test window — one went from
40–68 adoptions a month to 2. Training on them would have produced what looks
like model failure but is product withdrawal.

**My first result was wrong, and the corrected one is better.** The model
initially beat the baseline by 71%. The baseline was ranking all five products
while the model ranked only eligible ones — an unfair comparison. Re-scored on
identical candidate sets, the lift is 22.4%. A 71% lift that survives no
scrutiny is worth less than a 22% lift that does.

## Data

[Santander Product Recommendation](https://www.kaggle.com/c/santander-product-recommendation)
— 17 monthly snapshots of real bank customers, Jan 2015 – May 2016. Sampled
50,000 customers, keeping every row for each.

**Real:** product-adoption behaviour, every evaluation number, the fairness
audit. **Synthetic:** consent flags, contact history, and the dollar value of
each action — the dataset has none of these. They exist to exercise the
decision path, not to produce findings, and are generated by a documented,
seeded process ([table in the case study](case-study.md#41-synthetic-inputs-declared)).

The line drawn: synthetic fields that **constrain** decisions are safe,
because they can only remove options. Synthetic fields that **feed the model
or carry an outcome** are not, and were left out — including prior action
outcomes, which would have enabled a feedback-loop analysis that was entirely
an artifact of the generator.

**Scope:** deposit, savings, payroll, and servicing products only. Credit and
lending are excluded — recommending credit touches fair-lending territory
(ECOA, disparate impact), and a prototype cannot handle that responsibly.
